12 Questions · ~60 Minutes

The Career Clarity Worksheet

Before you write another resume, send another email, or open another job board — answer these. Most people skip this part. That's why most people end up in the wrong place.

Question 01
What topics do you read about, watch videos on, or think about — even when no one's making you?
Not what you should be interested in. What you actually are.
Question 02
When was the last time you lost track of time doing something? What were you doing?
Flow states are clues. They point to where your attention naturally wants to live.
Question 03
In an ideal week, what does a "good day at work" look like? Not the title — the actual day.
Hours alone vs. with people. Deep focus vs. variety. Building things vs. talking to people. The texture matters more than the title.
Question 04
What do you want to be the deepest expert at? Or do you want to connect multiple domains?
Specialists go vertical. Generalists go horizontal. Both work. Knowing which one you are saves years.
Question 05
What kind of problems make you angry, energized, or impatient when you see them done badly?
The thing you can't stop noticing or critiquing — that's often where you'd be most useful.
Question 06
What's something you've taught yourself outside of school? Why?
Self-directed learning reveals what you actually care about — not what you were assigned.
Question 07
If money and status didn't exist, what would you spend your weeks doing?
This is a flawed question, but the answer points somewhere real. Take it seriously, even if the answer feels impractical.
Question 08
What's something you've already done that surprised people — including yourself?
Past evidence of capability you'd otherwise underrate. This is where confidence gets built.
Question 09
When you imagine yourself at 35, what does your work life look like? Not "successful" — specific.
Where do you live? What's your day? Who do you work with? "Doing well" is too vague to aim at.
Question 10
What kind of environments drain you? What kind energize you?
The wrong environment will break you in the right job. Most career regret is environment regret in disguise.
Question 11
What's a career path you've quietly considered but dismissed? Why did you dismiss it?
The reasons we dismiss things are often inherited, not chosen. Worth examining.
Question 12
If you could only build one skill over the next 3 years, which would compound the most?
Not the most interesting. The most leveraged. The one that opens doors to other doors.

10 Templates · Copy & Adapt

The Cold Outreach Playbook

The application portal is dead. The inbox is alive. Every offer in 2025 came from someone who got past the keyword filter and reached a human directly. These are the messages that worked.

Before you copy anything Replace every [bracket] with something specific to the person you're writing. Generic outreach gets ignored. Specific outreach gets answered. Personalization is the entire game.
Template 01
The Alumni LinkedIn DM
Use when: reaching out to graduates from your school working in your target field.
Hi [Name], I'm a current student at [your school] majoring in [major]. I came across your profile while researching [their company] — your path from [something specific from their bio] caught my attention because I'm trying to navigate something similar. I'm not asking for a job. I'd just love 15 minutes of your time to ask a few honest questions about how you got from where I am to where you are. I'll bring specific questions, respect your time, and never ask for anything beyond that conversation. Would any time in the next two weeks work? Best, [your name]
Why this works"Not asking for a job" disarms the most common reason alumni ignore students. Specificity (their bio, your major) signals you actually looked. The 15-minute frame makes saying yes low-cost. Most alumni want to help — they just don't trust most students to respect their time.
Template 02
The Recruiter Cold Email
Use when: you've identified a specific recruiter at a company you want to join.
Subject: [Specific role title] — quick question Hi [Name], I noticed [company] is hiring for [role], and I've already submitted an application — but I know recruiters get hundreds of those. So instead of just hoping mine gets seen, I wanted to give you 90 seconds of context that the resume can't: — I built [specific project relevant to the role] last year, which directly maps to [a responsibility in the JD]. — I chose [company] because [a specific reason — recent product, founder talk, mission, anything genuine]. Happy to send a 2-minute Loom walking through the project, or jump on a quick call if it helps. Either way, thanks for reading. [your name]
Why this worksAcknowledges the noise problem upfront. Gives concrete, specific signal in two bullets. Offers an asymmetric next step (Loom = low effort for them, high signal). Doesn't beg.
Template 03
The Hiring Manager Direct
Use when: you've identified the actual person who would manage you, not the recruiter.
Subject: [Specific value or insight] for the [team] team Hi [Name], I've been following [their team / product / area] and noticed [specific observation — a recent launch, a hiring trend, a public challenge]. I'm graduating in [date] and applying for [role] on your team. Rather than send another resume into the void, I put together [a one-pager / a short doc / a Loom] on [a specific problem the team likely cares about]. It's not a sales pitch — it's me showing you how I think. If it's useful, great. If not, no harm done. Link: [link] [your name]
Why this worksTreats the hiring manager like a peer, not a gatekeeper. Provides asymmetric value (a doc) before asking for anything. Frames the worst-case as zero downside, which makes opening the email feel safe.
Template 04
The Coffee Chat Request
Use when: you want to learn from someone in a role you're considering.
Hi [Name], Quick context: I'm [your year and major], and I'm seriously considering [their type of role] as a path. Before I commit, I want to talk to people actually doing it — not just read job descriptions. Would you be open to 20 minutes on Zoom in the next few weeks? I'd ask you three things: 1. What's the part of the job you didn't expect when you started? 2. What kind of person actually thrives in this role? 3. If you were starting over, what would you do differently in your final year of college? That's it. No pitch, no ask. Just trying to make a smarter decision before I make it. Thanks for considering, [your name]
Why this worksThe three questions are stated upfront — no mystery, no hidden agenda. People say yes because they can already see what the conversation looks like. Also: these three questions are genuinely useful. Use them.
Template 05
The Internship Inquiry
Use when: applying to small companies or startups where the founder might read it.
Subject: Internship — would love to help with [specific area] Hi [Name], I know you're not actively recruiting interns. I'm writing anyway because [company] is one of the few places where I think I'd actually learn fast — and where I might actually be useful. About me in three lines: — Currently studying [major] at [school], graduating [date]. — Last year I [specific project or accomplishment]. — I've been following [company] since [when / why]. I'd work on [specific problem you noticed they have]. Paid or unpaid, full-time or part-time, in person or remote — I'm flexible. If you have 15 minutes for a call, I'd love to make a case for myself. [your name] [link to portfolio / GitHub / writing]
Why this worksAcknowledges the no-formal-process reality of small companies. Three-line bio respects their time. Shows you noticed a real problem. Founders read these. Big company recruiters don't.
Template 06
The Two-Week Follow-Up
Use when: you applied or messaged and got no response. Send 10–14 days later.
Subject: Re: [original subject] Hi [Name], Following up on the message below. I know inboxes are chaos this time of year — no offense if it didn't make the cut. One thing I forgot to mention last time: [a small new piece of relevant info — a recent project, a news item about their company, a small achievement]. Open to chatting if there's still interest, or to hearing "not now" so I can stop checking my inbox. Either way, appreciate the time. [your name]
Why this worksThe "stop checking my inbox" line is honest, slightly self-deprecating, and gives them permission to say no — which paradoxically makes saying yes easier. Adding a small new fact justifies the follow-up without feeling pushy.
Template 07
The Polite Application Nudge
Use when: you've applied through a portal, want to flag your application directly to a human.
Hi [Name], Just submitted an application for [role] earlier today. Since I know portal applications often disappear into the abyss, I wanted to introduce myself directly. In one sentence: I'm a [year / major] student who built [specific thing] and chose to apply to [company] because of [specific reason]. Happy to share more, send a portfolio, or just stay on your radar if the timing isn't right yet. Thanks for taking a look, [your name]
Why this worksDoesn't ask for special treatment — just visibility. The "stay on your radar" close gives the recipient an easy out while keeping a door open. Recruiters appreciate this — it's professional, brief, and zero-pressure.
Template 08
The Networking Event Follow-Up
Use when: you met someone at an event and want to keep the connection alive.
Hi [Name], Great meeting you at [event] — your point about [specific thing they said] stuck with me. I went down a rabbit hole on it after and ended up reading [article / paper / source], which made me think about [your own take]. Curious what you'd push back on. Also: if it's ever useful to grab 20 minutes and talk shop, I'd love that. [your name]
Why this worksSpecificity ("your point about X") proves you were actually listening. Sharing what you read after shows intellectual engagement. The ask is buried at the end and easy to ignore — which is exactly why people say yes.
Template 09
The Referral Request
Use when: someone you know works at a company you want to join.
Hi [Name], Hope you're doing well — [brief personal note if you actually know them]. A direct ask: I'm applying to [role] at [company], and I'd be grateful if you'd be willing to refer me internally. I've attached my resume and a short note explaining why I'm a fit. Two things to make this easier: 1. Zero pressure — if you don't feel comfortable referring me, I 100% understand. 2. If you do, I'll write the entire referral note for you to copy/paste/edit. You wouldn't have to think about it. Either way, appreciate you reading. [your name]
Why this worksMost referral asks fail because they make the ask too vague and too costly. This one is specific (which role) and removes friction (you'll write the note). The "zero pressure" line preserves the relationship even if they say no.
Template 10
The Post-Interview Standout
Use when: within 24 hours of an interview. Most candidates send a generic thank-you. Don't.
Subject: Thanks — and one thought I forgot to mention Hi [Name], Genuinely enjoyed our conversation today — especially the part about [something specific they said]. After we hung up, I kept thinking about [a question they asked or topic that came up]. Here's the cleaner version of what I should have said: [2–3 sentences with a sharper or more thoughtful answer than what you gave in real time] That said — appreciate the time and your candor. If there's anything else I can send (work samples, references, anything), happy to. [your name]
Why this worksWhile every other candidate sends "Thanks for your time! Looking forward to next steps!" — you send something that proves you've kept thinking. It's the difference between forgettable and memorable. Use it sparingly, only when you genuinely have a sharper thought.
